Pass@1 And Pass@k

Imagine you ask an AI model to solve a hard math problem. Pass@1 is: you get exactly one answer and check if it's right. Pass@k is: you tell the model to generate k different answers (say 5, or 100), then check if ANY of them are right. Pass@k shows whether the model *can* find the right answer if given more tries, even if its first attempt fails.

First Principles

When you give a model the same problem twice, it usually answers differently—sometimes it picks the right path, sometimes wrong. Pass@1 measures: how often does the first attempt happen to work? Pass@k measures: if we give the model many separate attempts, what fraction of the time does at least one path lead to the right answer? The gap between them tells you whether the model has the answer buried inside—just not always on the surface.

Concrete Example

Suppose a model solves a coding problem with pass@1 = 40% (gets it right 4 out of 10 times) but pass@100 = 90% (at least one correct solution in 100 tries, 90 out of 100 times). This means: the model *knows* how to solve it most of the time, but its first guess often picks a dead-end path. Now imagine another model with pass@1 = 70% and pass@100 = 71%. This model is reliable—most first tries work—but it thinks the same narrow way every time.

Why It Matters

In reasoning models, we're trying to build AI that thinks step by step and improves itself. Pass@k tells us something pass@1 alone hides: is the model stuck in one rigid way of thinking, or does it have multiple solutions it could pick from? If pass@k jumps much higher than pass@1, we know the model is *capable* but just needs better guidance on which path to take first. If they're close, the model has already locked in its best behavior. This gap is how we know whether a model needs more reasoning time (more attempts) or needs training that teaches a better first instinct.

Common Misunderstanding

People sometimes think: higher pass@1 means the model is smarter. But pass@1 and pass@k measure different things. A model could have low pass@1 but high pass@k (thinks many different ways; needs to try more to land on the answer) or high pass@1 with nearly the same pass@100 (stuck in one confident way, even if wrong). The number that matters depends on your question: How much effort can you spend (one answer vs. 100)? And what's the real failure: Is the model unable to solve it at all, or just not sure which of its solutions is best?
