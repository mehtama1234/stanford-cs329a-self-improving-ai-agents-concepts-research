Mechanisms Versus Heuristics

A model's reasoning has two separate parts: the *tools it knows how to use* (mechanisms), and the *decision about which tool to pull out when* (heuristics). A calculator is a mechanism—it can add and divide. Knowing *when* to add versus divide is the heuristic. Training might not teach the model new tools; it might just teach the model to pick the right tool at the right time.

First Principles

A large language model is a program with billions of tuning dials that predicts the next word. Inside, it can already perform certain operations: it can restate a problem differently, check if an answer makes sense, split a hard question into smaller pieces, or erase a mistake and try again. These built-in moves are mechanisms. When you train the model further with examples of good reasoning (a process called reinforcement learning, where a score rewards correct answers), the model may not learn *new* ways to think—instead, it learns to use its existing toolkit more often, or to pick the right tool for the right moment. This is the heuristic: the internal choice of when to activate which mechanism.

Concrete Example

Imagine a student who already knows how to check their math by plugging the answer back into the problem. At first, they rarely do this check—they just write down an answer and move on. Then, after doing practice problems and seeing which checking moves save them from mistakes, they get better at *remembering to check* on the types of problems where it matters. They didn't learn a new skill (the checking mechanism was already there). They learned a heuristic: *when to apply what they already know*. The same model is true for AI reasoning. A base model (the model before extra training) may already know how to verify equations. Reinforcement learning can teach it to verify *before finalizing the answer on math problems*—not because verification is new, but because the model learned the heuristic: *verify when solving math*.

Why It Matters

This distinction splits into two competing ideas about how reasoning models improve: (1) the model learns entirely new reasoning moves (new mechanisms), or (2) the model already has the reasoning moves built in, and training teaches it *when and how often to use them* (the heuristic). The answer changes how we think about what base models can do, what training does, and whether a reasoning model is mostly discovering hidden skills or mostly learning to coordinate known skills. If most improvement comes from better heuristics, not new mechanisms, then base models are smarter than they appear—they just need practice picking the right move for the right moment.

Common Misunderstanding

When RL training makes a behavior happen more often (for example, the model checks its work twice as often as before), it is easy to assume RL *invented* that behavior—that the model couldn't check before. But an increase in frequency does not prove a mechanism is new. The model may have always been able to check; it just learned when checking pays off. Mistaking a frequency shift for a new ability can lead to false claims about what base models know and what training teaches.
