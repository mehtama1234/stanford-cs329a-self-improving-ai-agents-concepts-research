Muon Optimizer

When a neural network learns, it adjusts billions of tiny dials (called parameters or weights) by looking at how wrong its guesses are. An optimizer is a recipe that decides which dials to turn and by how much. Muon is one such recipe — it treats the dials like a grid of numbers (a matrix) and rotates or stretches updates together, instead of fiddling with each dial separately like the popular AdamW method does.

First Principles

Start with the core problem: after each training step, you measure how wrong the model was (the gradient tells you which direction to nudge each dial). A naive approach is to nudge each dial a little bit in the direction that reduces error. But dials are not independent — some form groups that work together (like the weights in a layer of a transformer that processes words). Muon notices this: instead of tuning each dial alone, it treats groups as rigid units and rotates or scales them together. This can be smoother and more stable — like steering a car by turning the wheels together rather than jiggling each one.

Concrete Example

Imagine training a transformer on a large text dataset. In one experiment, Muon-style updates — which respect the grid structure of weight matrices — let the model reach the same quality in fewer training steps than AdamW, which treats each weight as a separate thing to adjust. Or imagine a layer with 1,000 weights: AdamW might learn different step sizes for weight 1, weight 2, weight 3, etc.; Muon might say all weights in that layer move together in a coordinated rotation, which sometimes reduces wasted effort.

Why It Matters

Training a big model costs millions of dollars in computer time and energy. If a smarter optimizer recipe lets you reach the same model quality with fewer steps or less compute, that saves real money and carbon. Muon is interesting because it is not just tuning data or buying faster hardware — it is a new idea about how to nudge dials more wisely. If it works at the giant scale where real frontier models train, it could be an algorithmic win: a better idea, not a better GPU.

Common Misunderstanding

A better optimizer in one setting does not mean it will work everywhere. Maybe Muon shines when training a specific kind of model or dataset, but fails on another. Scale matters too: an optimizer that works well on a small experiment might break or give no gain when training a model with 100 billion dials. Architecture, the exact implementation, even the random seed — all of these affect whether Muon's coordinate-together idea actually helps. Calling Muon 'better than AdamW' without saying where and why is premature.
