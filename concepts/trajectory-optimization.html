Trajectory Optimization For Agents

Trajectory optimization means improving the whole path an agent takes, not just a single action or final answer.

Read the deep dive — seen on a real run →

Ordinary Problem

A task can fail because the sequence of steps is wrong even when some individual steps look reasonable.

Naive Picture

Optimize each step locally and the full task will work.

Why It Fails

A locally good action can lead to a dead end, miss information, or make later verification impossible.

First-Principles Move

Agent work has path dependence. The quality object is the trajectory: state, action, observation, revised state, and final result.

What Breaks Without It

The agent makes short-sighted moves that look useful but damage the long-run task.

Course Role

Part 5 discusses refining plans and trajectories after feedback.

Worked Example

A model is asked to solve "Find the pH of a 0.1M HCl solution." Trajectory 1: it uses the wrong formula initially (pH = -log[H+] is correct, but it applies pH = pKa + log([A-]/[HA])) and arrives at an incorrect answer. After feedback, it generates Trajectory 2: it first analyzes the problem (recognize strong acid), decomposes it into steps (strong acid fully dissociates, then compute pH from H+ concentration), applies the correct formula (pH ≈ 1), and verifies the answer. The second trajectory uses 85 tokens for the entire reasoning chain. During RL training, both trajectories are sampled from the model. Trajectory 1 receives reward R_1 = 0 (wrong answer). Trajectory 2 receives R_2 = 1 (correct). Using process-level gradients, the RL algorithm computes ∇log π(trajectory_2 | problem) weighted by advantage A_2 = R_2 - baseline ≈ 0.5 (after normalization), and ∇log π(trajectory_1) weighted by A_1 ≈ -0.5. The optimizer learns that the step-decomposition pattern and explicit formula check are higher-value decisions than shortcuts.

One Level Deeper

Trajectory optimization treats the entire sequence of reasoning steps + actions as the unit of optimization, not isolated decisions. The trajectory τ = (s_0, a_1, s_1, a_2, ..., s_T, outcome) is scored end-to-end by a reward model R(τ) ∈ [0, 1] or discrete outcomes (correct/incorrect, success/failure). During RL, the policy π(a | s) is trained to maximize expected trajectory return: ∇J = E_τ[∇log π(a_t | s_t) * A(τ)], where advantage A(τ) can be outcome-based (sparse: R(τ) - baseline) or process-based (dense: individual step scores from a process reward model). The key distinction from step-level optimization is that one bad action early in a trajectory can invalidate all subsequent steps, even if individually reasonable. Thus the trajectory loss is not decomposable—minimizing loss at step t without considering future steps t+1, ..., T can lead the model down a locally-optimal but globally-poor path. Process reward models address this by scoring intermediate states ν(s_t) ≈ P(correct_final_answer | history_to_t), and computing advantage as A(s_t) = ν(s_{t+1}) - ν(s_t), giving step-level credit assignment grounded in end-to-end trajectory quality.

Failure Boundary

Trajectory optimization fails when (a) path dependence is severe but poorly captured—if early choices foreclose future options, but the reward signal is sparse, the model cannot distinguish between "early mistake that ruins later work" and "late mistake despite good early setup"; credit assignment becomes impossible. (b) Trajectories are long and stochastic—as trajectory length T grows, variance in gradient estimates increases (high variance in advantage A(τ) introduces noise), and any correlation between environment stochasticity and the sampled action becomes confounded with true gradient direction. (c) The process reward model itself is miscalibrated—if ν(s_t) is trained only on forward-pass data and never on backtracking or recovery paths, it learns to reward confident-sounding steps even if they dead-end; the trajectory optimizer then learns to mimic surface plausibility rather than logical correctness. (d) Multiple valid trajectories exist but the reward collapses them—if two different reasoning paths both lead to the correct answer (e.g., via different decompositions), but the reward model only recognizes one, the optimizer learns to converge on that single path, losing diversity and robustness. This is especially damaging in novel or out-of-distribution problems where the unrecognized path may be the only one that generalizes.
