Pretraining

Pretraining is the first, enormous learning phase. A model — a program with billions of dials (numbers) it can adjust — reads through mountains of books, articles, websites, and code. For each piece of text, it plays a guessing game: given what came before, predict the next word. It repeats this game millions of times, tweaking its dials whenever it guesses wrong, until it becomes very good at guessing what comes next. Through this simple game, it sponges up language, facts, how things are explained, and patterns in how people think and write.

First Principles

Nobody tells the model 'be helpful' or 'solve algebra problems' during pretraining. Instead, the model only sees one rule: predict the next word given the words before it. To win this game, it must build internal shortcuts and patterns — a model of grammar, of what makes sense, of how concepts fit together, of mathematical tricks, of how code works, of when arguments are strong or weak. Think of it like learning a language by reading millions of books: you never memorize them, but you internalize how language works. The model does this with numbers instead of intuition, but the principle is the same.

Concrete Example

Imagine the model reads '2x + 3 = 7. To solve, subtract 3 from both sides to get 2x = 4. Then divide by 2 to get x = 2.' This appears thousands of times in math explanations across the internet. The model starts to notice a pattern: when you see an algebra problem, the next words often describe a transformation step. After reading many examples, the model has internalized a hidden understanding of how algebra works — not because it memorized one lesson, but because it saw the pattern repeated across contexts.

Why It Matters

Everything that comes later — teaching the model to be helpful, training it to reason step-by-step, rewarding it for good answers, building it into an agent that learns from its own mistakes — all of that sits on top of pretraining. If pretraining has not already built useful internal patterns and structure, later training has almost nothing to work with. Pretraining is the foundation. Without a strong foundation, no amount of fine-tuning fixes a weak base.

Common Misunderstanding

Many people think pretraining is just memorizing text — the model reads a book and stores it. That is partly true, but it misses the point. Pretraining does include some memorization (names, famous quotes), but the real value is learning to compress: finding the repeating structure underneath. A child does not memorize every conversation but learns the rules of grammar and speech from hearing many examples. The model does the same — it keeps the patterns and rules, and discards the exact words. This compression is what lets the model handle new situations it has never seen.
