Distillation

Distillation is teaching one AI model to copy what another AI model does — the goal is to move a skill from one model into a new model that is cheaper to run, smaller, or better at one specific task. Instead of learning from scratch with raw text, the new model learns by watching how the skilled model solves problems.

First Principles

Imagine a student who watches a tutor solve hard problems step-by-step. The student doesn't just get told "right" or "wrong" — they see the whole reasoning path: the mistakes the tutor notices, the approach the tutor picks, the checks the tutor does. The student trains by copying and memorizing these observed steps. The original model (the tutor) generates outputs, shows its work, writes down its preferences (this path was better than that one), or gives full worked solutions. The new model (the student) tunes its dials by trial and error until its own answers match what it saw the teacher produce. The student may end up doing things it could never find on its own from raw data alone.

Concrete Example

A powerful reasoning model learns to solve hard math by trying thousands of problems and getting rewarded when it finds the right answer. A smaller, faster model is then trained to copy that reasoning: the smaller model reads many solved problems, watches the reasoning traces (the step-by-step work), and adjusts itself to produce similar reasoning. Now the smaller model is much better at math tasks than it would be if trained from scratch, because it saw explicit, high-quality solutions instead of having to discover them.

Why It Matters

Teaching a model by showing it worked solutions is faster and often more effective than only rewarding it with "right" or "wrong" scores. With a score alone, the model has to guess how to get there; with a full solution, the model sees the path. Distillation also lets you shrink a large, expensive model down to a small one that runs fast on a phone or embedded device, so the reasoning and step-by-step thinking that once required a server can now happen anywhere. This matters for reasoning models because the step-by-step work is the whole point — you want that detailed reasoning available even in a smaller, deployed system.

Common Misunderstanding

Many think distillation means the student model must be smaller than the teacher. That is not true. A larger model can also learn and improve by studying the outputs and reasoning traces a different teacher model made, even if the teacher is smaller. The goal is not shrinkage — the goal is transferring a skill. The student gets better at a task because it learned from high-quality examples, not because it was made compact.
