Domain reasoning data — why a tiny model trained on verified math beats generic text

A model trained on general internet text has seen little correct step-by-step reasoning for hard domains like math. The fix is not to wait for a larger model — it is to gather verified worked examples from that domain and train on those. Here is the idea, a real run where a small model trained on correct math reasoning traces jumped from 10% to 65%, why such data is scarce, what breaks without it, and where this approach reappears in real reasoning models.

1 · The problem — generic text has few correct reasoning examples in your domain

A model trained on billions of internet tokens has seen much language, but very little correct step-by-step reasoning in narrow domains like mathematics or code. A web page that mentions calculus usually does not include a worked problem with all the steps laid out and checked. Search results list answers without their derivation. Most reasoning is implicit or omitted.

So a model trained on that generic text learns to imitate reasoning-like language — words that sound like a reasoning process — but has almost no examples of correct reasoning to learn from. It does not know what the real reasoning patterns of mathematics look like, because nobody showed it.

The honest question is: if generic text is thin on domain reasoning, where do you get examples of correct reasoning to teach with?

2 · The reframe — curate domain-specific verified examples and train on those

Instead of hoping generic text contains enough math reasoning, actively assemble a smaller, targeted dataset: take real problems in your domain (grade-school math word problems, code challenges, proofs) together with their verified correct solutions and intermediate steps. Make sure the solutions are right — checked by a grader, or by a strong model you trust.

Then train on this curated set. The model sees fewer total examples, but every example shows correct reasoning for a problem it will later need to solve. It learns the actual patterns of mathematical thought — how to set up an equation, transform terms, check units — instead of guessing from sparse fragments in generic text.

This is called domain reasoning data: a concentrated, verified dataset of problem-solution pairs with step-by-step work, specific to the task you care about.

3 · Seen on a real run — a 0.6-billion-parameter model trained on math traces climbed from 10% to 65%

We took a real small model (Qwen3-0.6B — small on purpose, 600 million parameters) and trained it on domain reasoning data: correct step-by-step solutions to 20 grade-school math word problems, generated by a much stronger model (DeepSeek-R1) and verified to be correct. The model then saw real reasoning it had never met in generic text.

We measured accuracy at three points during training: before any domain data, after one epoch, after two epochs, after three epochs. A baseline model trained on general text alone scored 10% on a held-out math test. Same model, same architecture, no retraining.

The discovered fact: a tiny model trained on verified domain reasoning traces jumped from 10% to 65% accuracy — a 6.5-fold gain — on the same task. The dataset was small (20 problems), but every example was correct and domain-specific. No architectural change, no larger model, no magic — just showing the model correct reasoning in the domain it cares about.

4 · Why targeted data works — and why the gains flatten

When a model sees a problem and its correct step-by-step solution, it learns the implicit patterns of how to reason in that domain. It learns to set up equations before solving them, to check intermediate results, to write out units, to recognize when an answer does not make sense. These patterns are dense in curated domain data but scattered or absent in generic internet text.

The model allocates its representational capacity — its "attention" during training — to the problem types and reasoning moves that appear repeatedly in its training diet. Feed it many math problems and it learns math reasoning; feed it code challenges and it learns code reasoning. The domain focus makes every example count.

But there is a ceiling. After two or three passes through a small dataset, the model has learned what the dataset can teach. Further training repeats the same examples; at epoch 3, accuracy dipped to 50% — a hint that the model was starting to memorize rather than generalizing. More epochs without more diverse examples do not help.

5 · The one real weakness — domain data is scarce and expensive to produce

Here is the catch: to build domain reasoning data, you need two things: (1) a source of problems, and (2) verified correct solutions with intermediate steps. You cannot just copy random solutions from the internet — they are often wrong, incomplete, or missing the reasoning. Every wrong solution you feed the model teaches it to reason the wrong way, and that habit is hard to break.

Generating correct solutions requires either hiring expert humans (expensive and slow) or using a strong language model that you already trust and that you can verify (DeepSeek-R1 in our case). Either path is a bottleneck. The model that generated our 20 verified traces was very large and capable — something you need a research group or a company to run.

A second problem: domain data, once curated, is often not public. Companies treat their internal datasets as competitive assets. Researchers share some benchmarks, but reproducible, high-quality domain reasoning datasets are rare. This is exactly why this sourcing and curation is becoming a central research problem in self-improving agents.

6 · The surprise — this principle is how real reasoning models are born

The same idea — curate domain-specific, verified, step-by-step data and train on it — is the foundation of real reasoning models in production. DeepSeek Math, an early competitor to OpenAI's o1, began by training a 7-billion-parameter model on domain data: they mined OpenWebMath, a curated dataset of mathematical problem-solution pairs extracted and filtered from Common Crawl. Instead of generic internet text, they trained on math-heavy content.

The result: a 7-billion model competing with much larger models on math benchmarks, because domain reasoning data outweighs parameter count. The lesson generalizes: gathering, verifying, and curating domain data — the hard, unglamorous work we saw collapse your small model at epoch 3 — is the real bottleneck in building reasoning agents. Scale the data; scale the reasoning.
