Test-Time Compute

Test-time compute means spending extra computation while using a fixed model to improve the final answer.

Read the deep dive — seen on a real run →

Ordinary Problem

A model may know how to solve a problem sometimes, but one attempt may land on a weak path.

Naive Picture

One model call measures the model's full ability.

Why It Fails

Generation is variable. A single answer may miss correct paths that appear under repeated attempts, search, or refinement.

First-Principles Move

If the model's answer distribution contains good candidates, then more inference can sample or search that distribution. The gain appears only if the system can choose or verify better candidates.

What Breaks Without It

You underuse capable models or waste runtime compute without a selection rule.

Course Role

Part 2 develops this as a major self-improvement mechanism.

Worked Example

The "Large Language Monkeys" paper shows this concretely. Take Llama 3-70B, which with one attempt (pass@1) solves 20% of hard math problems—worse than GPT-4o's single-attempt 45%. But generate 1,000 samples from Llama 3-70B and use a verifier to select correct ones: now it solves 60% of problems, beating GPT-4o. No retraining, no new model weights. The entire 40-point gain comes from *test-time compute*: 1,000 inference calls instead of 1.

Another example from the transcript: reasoning models like o1 show log-linear scaling. On AIME (hard math), accuracy improves from ~30% to ~50% as test-time compute increases 10x. This is measured in inference tokens spent, not model parameters. The cost is latency and compute per query, but the capability gain is substantial without touching the pre-trained model.

One Level Deeper

Test-time compute works because generation is stochastic. The model's output distribution P(answer | question) contains correct answers at non-zero probability, but not always at the highest probability. Repeated sampling increases the chance of drawing a correct answer at least once.

Formally, if pass@1 = p (probability a single sample is correct for a problem), then pass@k (probability at least one of k samples is correct) = 1 - (1-p)^k. Across a dataset with heterogeneous pass@1 values, the lecture shows coverage follows a power law: Coverage ≈ a · k^(-b) where k is the number of samples and a, b are fitted parameters. This power law holds empirically across models from 70M to 70B parameters, suggesting it is a fundamental property of the sampling distribution.

The verifier's role is critical: it must accurately score each sample and select or aggregate the best ones. Outcome reward models (ORM) score final answers; process reward models (PRM) score intermediate steps. A perfect verifier with perfect selection gives pass@k = 1 when the correct answer exists in the sample set.

Failure Boundary

Test-time compute cannot help if the model's distribution does not contain correct answers (pass@1 = 0 for all samples). This occurs outside the model's training distribution or for tasks requiring knowledge the model entirely lacks.

A second boundary: weak or miscalibrated verifiers. If a verifier confidently selects an incorrect answer from the pool (or rejects a correct one), test-time scaling becomes harmful. The lecture shows a gap between coverage (if a perfect verifier existed) and what methods like majority voting achieve: for hard problems, majority voting plateaus after 10–50 samples because correct answers are rare (appearing 1–3 times in 10,000 samples), making frequency-based selection fail. This is the "generation-verification gap." Without an accurate verifier, additional samples do not monotonically improve performance.
