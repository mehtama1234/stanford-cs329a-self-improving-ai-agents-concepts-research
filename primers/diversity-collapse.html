Diversity Collapse

Imagine a model that can solve a problem 10 different ways. During training, if you reward only the way that works most often, the model gradually forgets the other 9 ways — even if some of them sometimes solve harder versions of the problem. Diversity collapse is when a model learns to repeat fewer and fewer answer patterns, because it got trained to copy what was rewarded most. The model becomes narrower, more predictable, and stops trying anything unusual.

First Principles

Here is how this happens: the training process nudges the dials (those billions of little numbers inside the model) to make rewarded answers more likely to appear next time. The catch: the training signal only says 'good' or 'bad' for the answers it sees. If only one type of answer gets marked 'good', the model turns up the dial that leads to that type, and turns down all the others. If no reward signal says 'try something different', the model has no reason to keep the rare paths alive. Over time, the model concentrates its probability onto a smaller set of familiar winning strategies and forgets the rare ones.

Concrete Example

A math solver learns to prove algebra problems in 10 different ways. For 6 months, only one proof style gets marked correct in training (say, substitution). The model's training nudges it to do substitution more and more. By the end, the model almost always tries substitution first — and forgets how to do the other 9 styles. Then you test it on a twisted problem where substitution doesn't work, but factoring (which the model has now forgotten) would work. The model fails, even though it knew factoring before.

Why It Matters

This matters because it explains a real trade-off in reasoning models. You can make a model more reliable at one type of problem (better pass@1: more correct on the first try) but worse at solving rare or unusual cases (lower pass@k: worse if you ask for many attempts and pick the best). It also breaks models on new kinds of problems they have not seen before. A reasoning model that only knows one path is brittle — it works until the problem changes slightly. Keeping diversity means keeping options in reserve.

Common Misunderstanding

Not all narrowing is bad. For repetitive or high-stakes tasks, having the model do the same safe thing every time can be exactly what you want. Surgeons do not want robots to experiment. The real problem is throwing away the rare paths that were actually useful. The goal is to stay narrow where narrowness is safe and wide where width solves harder problems.
