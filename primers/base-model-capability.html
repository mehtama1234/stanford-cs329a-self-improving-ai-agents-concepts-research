Base Model Capability

When you ask an AI language model — a program with billions of dials it tunes by trial and error — to solve a hard problem, one attempt might look bad. But the model actually contains many different ways it could have answered. If you ask it the same question 100 times, it picks differently each time (like rolling dice), and some attempts will be completely right. The model's real skill is hiding in those other paths it could take, not just the first answer it gives.

First Principles

A language model learns by guessing the next word over and over until it gets good at it. What it really learns is not a single right answer, but a ranked list — a spread of possibilities, each with a score for how likely it is. When you ask a question, the model doesn't pick the #1 answer every time; it treats the list like weighted dice. Roll once, you might get a mediocre answer. Roll 100 times, you might hit the correct one that was always possible but just rare. Capability lives in the whole ranked list, not just the top pick.

Concrete Example

Imagine the model sees a math problem: 'A train leaves at 3 PM going 60 miles per hour.' On its first answer, it makes an arithmetic slip and gets 180 miles. You ask again (with dice-roll randomness), and this time it carefully works through it step by step and gets 240 miles correctly. On 100 attempts, maybe 35 are right, 40 are close, and 25 are wrong. The skill to solve it correctly was always in the model; it just didn't come out first.

Why It Matters

This idea shows why you don't need to retrain or redesign the model to make it smarter. You can ask it many times and pick the best answer, or use a checker that grades answers and keeps only the good ones (called 'test-time compute' — spending more thinking time at the moment you ask, not earlier). The model's weights stay the same; you just extract the hidden good paths that were already there. It's like having a player who can make a tricky shot one-in-three times, and discovering that instead of asking for one shot, you let them try five times and keep the best one.

Common Misunderstanding

If the model gives you a wrong answer, it does not mean the model cannot do the task. It might just mean the dice roll came up unlucky, or the default way it picks answers (always the #1 option) missed the rare good one. Testing the real skill takes patience — asking many times, or using a checker — not just one try.
