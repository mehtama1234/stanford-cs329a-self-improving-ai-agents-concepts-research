Repeated sampling — when one careful try loses to eight noisy ones

A hard problem may have a right answer the model only stumbles onto now and then. The fix is not a better single attempt — it is many attempts and a vote. Here is the idea, a real run on a small model where voting beats the careful answer, why the gains flatten, when it fails, and where it reappears as pass@k and best-of-N.

1 · The problem — one attempt can land on a bad path

Ask a model a hard question once and it commits to a single chain of steps. If that chain takes a wrong turn early, the whole answer is wrong — even when the model, on a different day, would have gotten it. For some problems the right answer is not what the model says most of the time; it is something it only reaches on a lucky path.

So the honest question is not ‘how do I get the one perfect attempt?’ It is: if the right answer only shows up sometimes, how do I give it a chance to show up at all — and then recognise it?

2 · The reframe — sample many answers, then let them vote

Instead of one careful answer, take the same question and answer it many times with a little randomness turned on, so each try explores a different chain of reasoning. Now you have a pile of candidate answers. Most hard problems have one right answer and many different wrong ones — and the wrong ones tend to disagree with each other, while the right one tends to repeat. So count the final answers and keep the one that showed up most often. That count-and-keep-the-most-common move is called self-consistency, and how often the right answer appears at all across your tries is its coverage.

3 · Seen on a real run — eight noisy tries beat one careful one

We ran a real 0.6-billion-parameter model (Qwen3-0.6B — small on purpose, so it is genuinely unsure) on 14 grade-school math word problems. For each one we took the single careful answer, and separately drew 8 sampled answers with the randomness turned up, then voted. Every number here is measured.

how we answeraccuracy on the 14 problems
one careful (greedy) answer6 / 14 = 43%
one random sampled answer (average)39%
majority vote of 8 sampled answers8 / 14 = 57%
The discovered fact: many tries plus a vote beat one careful try — 43% to 57% — with no change to the model. A single random answer (39%) is noisier than the careful one (43%), so more randomness alone is not the point. The point is aggregation: across 8 tries the right answer surfaces often enough to win the vote. And the curve flattens after 6 paths, so the boost is real but bounded.

4 · Why voting wins — and why it stops helping

Wrong answers scatter; right answers cluster. If the model reaches the correct number on even a third of its tries, the correct number is usually the single most common one in the pile, because the mistakes spread themselves across many different wrong numbers. Voting turns ‘sometimes right’ into ‘usually picked.’

But there is a ceiling. If the right answer never appears in any of the tries — coverage is zero — no amount of voting can conjure it. That is why the curve above flattens at 57%: beyond a handful of paths, the extra tries just re-draw answers the vote has already seen. More samples widen coverage only until the model has shown you everything it can reach.

5 · The one real weakness — voting can only pick what sampling found

Self-consistency has no idea what is true; it only knows what is popular. If the model is confidently and consistently wrong — the same mistake on most paths — the vote will faithfully return that wrong answer, and more samples make it more sure, not more right. And every extra try costs real time and money, so you are paying more compute for a boost that flattens.

This is exactly why the next ideas in the course exist: a verifier to judge answers rather than just count them, and a way to spend an inference budget wisely instead of sampling forever.

6 · The surprise — this is pass@k, best-of-N, and the cheapest boost there is

The same move wears many names. Counting whether the right answer appears in any of k tries is pass@k, the standard way people measure a model's reachable ceiling. Keeping the best of N sampled answers by some score is best-of-N. Voting among them is self-consistency. All three are the same bet: spend more compute at answer-time, across independent tries, and aggregate.

And it needs no training at all — it is the cheapest, first thing to reach for when a fixed model underperforms. It is also the raw material every heavier method feeds on: a verifier picks from these samples, and reinforcement learning rewards the good ones. Get many tries first; everything else is about choosing among them.

7 · Where it is used in practice — Many tries plus voting beats one careful answer

Self-consistency is how o1, Llama-Reasoner, and smaller models handle uncertainty on hard problems: sample the same question many times with randomness enabled, extract the final answer from each trace, and vote. If the right answer surfaces on even a few paths, the vote tends to find it. This is used in production math graders, coding evaluators, and any system where you can afford to ask the model the same question multiple times and aggregate the results. Pass@k metrics measure this directly: did the right answer appear in any of your k independent tries?

The mechanics are simple: draw independent samples at high temperature to explore different reasoning paths, then tally which final answer appears most often. Wrong answers scatter across many different wrong numbers; right answers cluster. This makes voting robust when the model has the capability to solve the problem but is being noisy. The vote exploits the fact that consensus usually points to correctness, especially when wrong answers disagree with each other.

Voting hits a hard ceiling: if the right answer never appears in any path, no amount of voting can conjure it, and the gains flatten as you discover the model's full reachable space.
