Process Reward Models

A process reward model scores intermediate reasoning steps so training can reward good process, not just good final answers.

Ordinary Problem

A final reward is sparse and may arrive too late to show which step helped or hurt.

Naive Picture

Reward only the final output because that is the result users ultimately care about.

Why It Fails

Long chains contain many opportunities for small errors. A final reward does not locate them.

First-Principles Move

Dense step-level rewards give the training algorithm a clearer gradient toward valid reasoning behavior.

What Breaks Without It

RL can become unstable or teach superficial answer formatting instead of robust reasoning.

Course Role

Part 6 names process reward models as part of long-chain reasoning training.

Worked Example

On the AIME benchmark, a naive RL approach (GRPO applied directly) achieves 30% accuracy on Qwen-32B. When using a process reward model trained to score each intermediate step of reasoning in a math solution, the system generates multiple solution attempts and assigns a step-level reward r_t to each step (not just r_final). Illustrative: for a step-by-step algebra solution with 6 steps, the PRM might assign r_t values like [0.9, 0.85, 0.92, 0.1, 0, 0] indicating step 4 becomes incorrect.

The RL algorithm computes per-step gradients—when a step has r_t = 0.1 (partially aligned but wrong), the model learns what NOT to do at that specific state. By explicitly modeling step-level rewards instead of only rewarding correct final answers (r_final = 1 or 0), DAPO improved accuracy from 30% to 50% by: filtering out all-correct and all-wrong samples (keeping only those with mixed rewards between 0 and 64 samples), using asymmetric clipping to allow bigger probability increases, and applying token-level loss penalties on response length.

One Level Deeper

A process reward model is a learned function r_PRM(state_t, action_t) that outputs a scalar reward for each intermediate step t in a multi-step solution trajectory. During RL training, instead of receiving only a terminal reward r_final ∈ {0, 1} at the end, the agent gets a step-level signal. The advantage function in PPO-style algorithms then uses normalized advantage = (r_t - mean(rewards)) / std(rewards), where mean and std are computed over sampled trajectories for a single problem.

DAPO's dynamic sampling mechanism keeps only groups with at least one non-zero and non-one reward per problem—this ensures gradient variance is meaningful; if all sampled solutions are correct (all r_t = 1), the normalized advantage becomes undefined or zero. The PRM is trained on human annotations or on a held-out verification set to predict step-level quality.

At training time, each sampled solution generates a sequence of (state_t, action_t, r_t) tuples, and the policy loss is computed per-token instead of per-sample to account for variable reasoning chain length. Token-level aggregation prevents long incorrect reasoning chains from dominating the loss.

Failure Boundary

Process reward models fail when step-level annotations are unavailable or contradictory. In domains without clear intermediate verification (e.g., creative writing or open-ended reasoning), building a reliable PRM is impossible—there is no ground truth for 'is step 3 correct?' independent of the final answer. The PRM itself can be miscalibrated: if the reward model is overconfident (assigns r_t = 1 to actually-incorrect intermediate steps), the RL loop optimizes toward solutions the PRM incorrectly endorses, causing reward hacking.

The method also breaks when problems have multiple valid solution paths with different numbers of steps: a PRM trained on linear algebra might assign low rewards to valid but unconventional approaches. Additionally, PRMs require extensive training data themselves—annotating step correctness is more expensive than binary final-answer labels. If the PRM has high variance or low correlation with actual correctness, the advantage normalization becomes noisy and RL training becomes unstable.

Finally, the method assumes step independence (evaluating step t doesn't depend on steps 1 to t-1 being correct), but in reality, later steps may only make sense conditioned on prior correct steps; violating this assumption, the PRM assigns scores that don't align with the true solution structure.
