Repeated Sampling And Coverage

Repeated sampling asks the model many times and checks whether at least one attempt solves the problem.

Read the deep dive — seen on a real run →

Ordinary Problem

Some hard problems have rare correct outputs. You need a way to expose those rare successes.

Naive Picture

Sampling more is just randomness and therefore not principled.

Why It Fails

Sampling is useful when the model's distribution already contains correct candidates but does not reliably place them first.

First-Principles Move

Coverage asks whether the correct solution exists somewhere in the sampled set. Selection asks whether we can find it after sampling.

What Breaks Without It

You either stop too early after one miss or sample heavily without knowing how to identify success.

Course Role

This explains why test-time scaling and verification are inseparable.

Worked Example

On GSM8K (easier math problems), a 7B model has pass@1 = 35% with one sample. With repeated sampling: 8 samples gives pass@8 = 1 - (1 - 0.35)^8 ≈ 97% (assuming uniform difficulty). On MATH (harder problems), pass@1 = 3% for the same model. Now 8 samples gives pass@8 ≈ 1 - (1 - 0.03)^8 ≈ 22%—a dramatic difference in how many samples are needed.

The key insight from Large Language Monkeys: across 1,000 problems on MATH, some are easier (pass@1 = 20%) and some much harder (pass@1 = 0.5%). When you sample 1,000 times per problem and measure coverage (fraction of problems solved by at least one sample), you get a power-law curve. Smaller models like Llama-3-8B or even 70M-parameter models show the same scaling behavior, indicating it is model-agnostic.

One Level Deeper

Coverage is the empirical measurement of whether the correct answer appears in the sample set. For a dataset with problems of varying difficulty, the distribution of per-problem pass@1 values determines the power law. The paper shows that if you bin problems by pass@1 (easy: 0.8–1.0, medium: 0.4–0.8, hard: 0.1–0.4, very hard: 0.01–0.1, extremely hard: <0.01), the resulting *long tail of hard problems* justifies the power-law scaling.

Mathematically: Coverage(k) ≈ a - b · log(k) or Coverage(k) ≈ a · k^(-b) depending on the parametrization. The power law emerges because as k increases, you solve more easy problems (quickly saturating), then medium, then hard. The tail of extremely hard problems grows slowly, creating the sublinear curve. Process- and outcome-based selection both rely on this fundamental property: the correct answer must exist in the sample set before selection can succeed.

Failure Boundary

Repeated sampling with coverage breaks when the problem distribution lacks the long tail. If all problems have either pass@1 ≈ 0 or pass@1 ≈ 1 (binary difficulty), coverage plateaus immediately: doubling samples does not help on already-solved easy problems, and gains nothing on impossible hard ones.

Second, the assumption of statistical independence breaks down in structured domains. For example, if 8 samples are all variants of the same reasoning error (e.g., a systematic misunderstanding of a domain rule), they will all fail together. The transcript notes this happens in code generation: if the model has a misconception about a library API, it will make the same mistake in all 1,000 samples. Coverage then reflects the distribution of *distinct strategies*, not samples, and may require training or adversarial sampling to expand the space. Finally, verifying coverage requires a ground-truth oracle or a reliable verifier; without one, you cannot know whether the correct answer is actually in the set.
