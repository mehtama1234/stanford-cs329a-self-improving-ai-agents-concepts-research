Reasoning Traces

A reasoning trace is the visible or supervised path between the problem and the answer: steps, calculations, decisions, tool calls, and justifications.

Ordinary Problem

Hard tasks require linked intermediate states. If the model jumps directly to the answer, there is little to inspect or repair.

Naive Picture

Ask for a final answer and trust it if the prose sounds confident.

Why It Fails

A correct-looking final answer can hide bad intermediate logic, and a wrong answer gives no obvious clue about which step failed.

First-Principles Move

When a task is sequential, each intermediate step becomes input to the next. Exposing those steps gives the system something to supervise, verify, search over, or improve.

What Breaks Without It

Reasoning failure becomes invisible until the end, and feedback cannot be localized.

Course Role

This is the bridge from ordinary LLM prompting to reasoning models.

Worked Example

Classic chain-of-thought example from the lecture: "Roger has five tennis balls. He buys two more cans of tennis balls. Each can has three tennis balls. How many total tennis balls does he have now?" A naive model might output just "11" without explanation. With a reasoning trace, the model outputs: "Roger started with five balls. Two cans of three tennis balls is 6. 5 plus 6 is 11." The trace makes intermediate arithmetic explicit, allowing a human or verifier to catch errors at each step.

On harder problems, the lecture shows a 70B parameter model benefits from chain-of-thought on math datasets, but 7B and 8B models show almost no improvement without it. Only larger models (≥20B) reliably leverage provided reasoning traces. However, when a reasoning trace is present, even smaller models can follow it as a template for their own reasoning.

One Level Deeper

A reasoning trace is a sequence of intermediate representations: (s_0, s_1, s_2, ..., s_n) where s_0 is the input, each s_i depends on s_{i-1}, and s_n is the output. From an information-flow perspective: without a trace, the model must map directly from s_0 to s_n in one forward pass. With a trace, the model learns to factorize the problem: condition on s_i to predict s_{i+1}. Mathematically, this is autoregressive generation: P(answer | question, trace) can be supervised as P(s_i | s_0, s_1, ..., s_{i-1}).

In training, a supervised loss on step-level correctness is called process supervision (as opposed to outcome supervision, which only penalizes the final answer). The transcript notes that process supervision outperforms outcome supervision for long reasoning chains: it provides a denser signal, telling the model *which step* failed rather than only that the final answer was wrong. This is the foundation for process reward models (PRMs), which score each intermediate step.

Failure Boundary

Reasoning traces fail when intermediate steps are not independent or when the correctness of one step cannot be verified without understanding the whole problem. For example, in creative writing or open-ended design, intermediate "reasoning" steps are often post-hoc justifications rather than true causal steps.

Another boundary: if the model's latent reasoning distribution does not naturally produce correct intermediate steps, providing a trace template does not help much. The lecture shows that very small models (7B) do not benefit from chain-of-thought examples—they may lack the underlying capability to follow the reasoning pattern. Additionally, if verifiers or human annotators cannot reliably judge intermediate steps (e.g., in ambiguous domains), process supervision becomes noisy and unreliable, and the benefit of exposing steps disappears.
